Journal entries that make sense

About 50 minutes including written practice. Tutor-created examples unless identified as slide examples.

Repair debit/credit directions by reasoning from account type and change. Cover every Chapter 2 investing/financing transaction type used in class.

1. What we assume and measure

  • Separate entity: record the company's activities separately from the owner's personal spending.
  • Going concern: assume the business continues long enough to fulfil its plans and obligations.
  • Monetary unit: express measurable events in a currency; these course examples do not restate ordinary amounts for inflation.
  • Historical cost: initially measure assets at acquisition cost. Land bought for $60,000 stays at its recorded cost in an ordinary question even if its estimated selling price becomes $90,000. Apply an expressly taught exception, such as inventory write-downs, when the question calls for it.

A classified balance sheet separates current and non-current items. Use the slides' one-year convention: current assets are cash or expected to be used/converted within a year; current liabilities are due within a year. A note due in six months is current; a five-year note is non-current apart from any current portion. US examples list assets by liquidity and liabilities by maturity.

2. The recording system

A transaction is an event with a measurable effect on the statements. It can be external (buying supplies) or internal (using prepaid insurance). Merely signing an ordinary unperformed purchase agreement exchanges promises and usually creates no entry in this course.

An account accumulates increases and decreases for one item. A journal entry records one transaction's accounts, sides and amounts. The general journal stores entries chronologically. The general ledger groups postings by account. A T-account is the left/right representation of a ledger account. A trial balance lists account balances to check total debits equal credits; equal totals do not prove the right accounts were used.

3. Choose the account and change first

Debit means left; credit means right. Neither word alone tells you whether an account increases. Normal balance means the side on which the account ordinarily increases and holds its balance.

  • Assets and expenses: increases are debits; decreases are credits.
  • Liabilities, contributed capital, retained earnings and revenues: increases are credits; decreases are debits.
  • Treasury Stock: a contra-equity account with a debit balance; buying back shares increases this account and reduces total equity.
  • Accumulated Depreciation and Allowance for Doubtful Accounts: contra-assets with credit balances; they reduce the related asset's net amount.
  • Sales Discounts and Sales Returns: contra-revenues with debit balances. Dividends reduce RE; follow the slides' direct debit to RE.

Why do expenses increase on the debit side? Expenses reduce earnings and ultimately equity. Revenue increases earnings and ultimately equity, so revenue increases on the credit side.

Account → Type → Increase/decrease → Debit/credit → Amount → Check equality

Work through an equipment entry

On 1 October buy equipment for $12,000, paying $3,000 now and owing $9,000. Equipment is an asset increasing: debit. Cash is an asset decreasing: credit. Accounts Payable is a liability increasing: credit.

Oct 1
Dr Equipment                 12,000
    Cr Cash                            3,000
    Cr Accounts Payable                9,000

Total debits $12,000 = total credits $12,000. Net assets rise $9,000 and liabilities rise $9,000. Use Notes Payable when the question specifies a formal note.

4. All eight Chapter 2 transaction patterns

Read the event, then cover the entry and reproduce it. These are the class patterns; numerical practice below uses fresh amounts. For the lease example, the provided asset/liability amounts are enough: this lesson requires no lease valuation calculation.

(a) Issue 1,000 shares, $1 par, for $6 each
Dr Cash                       6,000
    Cr Common Stock                    1,000
    Cr Additional Paid-in Capital      5,000

(b) Borrow $4,000 under a note
Dr Cash                       4,000
    Cr Notes Payable                   4,000

(c) Buy equipment $2,600 and intangibles $500 for cash
Dr Equipment                  2,600
Dr Intangible Assets            500
    Cr Cash                            3,100

(d) Acquire land 20, buildings 40, lease asset 86 (class units)
Dr Land                          20
Dr Buildings                     40
Dr Operating Lease Assets        86
    Cr Cash                               29
    Cr Current Lease Liabilities           6
    Cr Long-term Lease Liabilities       111

(e) Sell investments at their $1,900 carrying amount
Dr Cash                       1,900
    Cr Short-term Investments          1,900

(f) Repay $1,000 note principal
Dr Notes Payable              1,000
    Cr Cash                            1,000

(g) Repurchase shares for $1,030
Dr Treasury Stock             1,030
    Cr Cash                            1,030

(h) Declare $200 dividends, payable later
Dr Retained Earnings            200
    Cr Dividends Payable                 200
Later payment:
Dr Dividends Payable            200
    Cr Cash                              200

Share proceeds above par go to APIC. Repurchases reduce equity through Treasury Stock. Declaration creates the dividend liability and reduces RE; later payment settles the liability. The equipment purchase and investment sale at cost create no immediate expense or gain.

5. Post to a T-account

Post each debit to the left and each credit to the right of its own account. Cash begins with a $5,000 debit balance, receives $4,000 from borrowing, and pays $3,000 for equipment: debits $9,000 minus credits $3,000 = $6,000 ending debit balance.

Cash
Debit (left)     | Credit (right)
Beginning 5,000  | Equipment 3,000
Loan      4,000  |
Ending debit balance = 6,000

Journal-entry presentation carries marks: include the date/reference; debit lines first; credit lines below and indented; total debits equal total credits. A balanced entry can still be wrong if it reverses the economic effects.

Try it without notes

1. Repair the original weakness

On 4 October buy equipment for $18,000, pay $5,000 cash and owe the supplier the rest. Write the dated entry and explain each direction.

Reveal solution and reasoning
Oct 4
Dr Equipment                 18,000
    Cr Cash                            5,000
    Cr Accounts Payable               13,000

Asset up: debit. Asset down: credit. Liability up: credit.

2. Separate principal from expense

Pay $2,300 to the bank: $2,000 note principal and $300 interest incurred this period. No interest was previously recorded. Write the entry.

Reveal solution and reasoning
Payment date
Dr Notes Payable              2,000
Dr Interest Expense             300
    Cr Cash                            2,300

Only $300 reduces current profit. Principal reduces an existing liability.

3. Share issuance and repurchase

Issue 2,000 shares at $8, par $1. Later repurchase shares for $2,500. Write both entries and calculate total change in equity from these events.

Reveal solution and reasoning
Issue date
Dr Cash                      16,000
    Cr Common Stock                    2,000
    Cr Additional Paid-in Capital     14,000
Repurchase date
Dr Treasury Stock             2,500
    Cr Cash                            2,500

Total equity rises $13,500; no sales revenue is earned.

4. Post and classify

A/P starts at $2,000 credit. Buy $4,000 supplies on account and pay suppliers $3,500. Find ending A/P. Classify a note due in eight months. Does a balanced trial balance detect posting an equipment purchase to Rent Expense?

Reveal solution and reasoning

A/P = $2,000 + $4,000 - $3,500 = $2,500 credit. Eight-month note: current liability. The wrong expense account can leave debits equal credits, so a trial balance cannot detect every error.
