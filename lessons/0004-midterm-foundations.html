The accounting picture

About 35 minutes including written practice. Tutor-created examples unless identified as slide examples.

Understand what accounting measures, who uses it, and how the four statements connect. These are the Chapter 1 concepts named in the guide.

Read one section, look away, and explain it aloud. For questions, write your answer before revealing the solution. The scratch boxes do not save; keep important workings on paper or send them in chat.

1. What accounting does

Accounting identifies and measures a business's economic activities, records their effects, and communicates the results to people making decisions. A useful report tells you what resources exist, who has claims on them, how the business performed, and how cash moved.

Financial accounting produces periodic financial statements and disclosures, mainly for external users such as investors and creditors. Investors ask about returns and risk; lenders ask whether principal and interest can be paid. Employees care about stability, suppliers about payment, customers about continuity, and governments about reporting and compliance. People inside the company also use financial statements.

Managerial accounting provides detailed internal information to run the business: budgets, product costs, staffing choices and make-or-buy decisions. It can focus on a department or the future and be produced whenever management needs it.

For this course, associate US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) with FASB and IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) with IASB. The slides describe Hong Kong's system as IFRS-based with local variations. These are frameworks for consistent reporting.

2. Three business activities

  • Operating: ordinary revenue-producing operations, such as selling meals, buying ingredients, paying employees and collecting customer receivables.
  • Investing: acquiring or selling long-term productive resources and investments, such as equipment, land or an investment holding.
  • Financing: obtaining or returning funding from owners and lenders: issuing shares, borrowing, repaying loan principal, repurchasing shares and paying dividends.

Ask what the transaction is for. Buying inventory supports ordinary sales; buying a machine builds the business's productive capacity. Both spend cash, but their purposes differ. Dividends distribute earnings to owners and are financing outflows.

3. Assets, liabilities and equity

Assets are controlled resources expected to provide future benefits: Cash, Accounts Receivable (customers owe us), Inventory (goods for sale), Supplies, Prepaid Rent, Equipment and Land. Liabilities are obligations: Accounts Payable (we owe suppliers), Wages Payable, Notes Payable and Unearned Revenue (we owe future service to customers).

Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity

Equity is the owners' residual claim: assets minus liabilities. In our basic model it consists of contributed capital plus retained earnings, with treasury stock reducing total equity when shares are repurchased. Contributed capital comes from owners; retained earnings comes from accumulated profits less dividends. Retained earnings is an equity balance; it does not identify a separate bank account.

4. Four statements, four questions

  • Balance sheet: what do we have and owe at a date? Assets, liabilities and equity; a snapshot.
  • Income statement: what did we earn and use up during a period? Revenues, expenses, gains/losses and net income. Common revenue titles: Sales, Service, Interest and Rent Revenue. Expense titles include COGS, Wages, Rent, Utilities, Insurance, Depreciation and Income Tax Expense.
  • Statement of stockholders' equity / retained earnings: why did owners' claims change during the period? Contributions, repurchases, income and dividends.
  • Statement of cash flows: why did cash change during the period? Operating, investing and financing receipts/payments.
Ending RE = Beginning RE + Net income - Dividends
Ending cash = Beginning cash + Net operating + Net investing + Net financing cash flows

In the course's simple examples, net income is revenues minus expenses, including any gains/losses in the appropriate subtotals. A loss reduces retained earnings. Share issuance increases contributed capital; it produces no revenue. A dividend reduces equity; it creates no expense.

Follow one business through all four statements

A new service business receives $10,000 from issuing shares, borrows $4,000, buys equipment for $6,000 cash, earns and collects $3,000 service revenue, pays $1,000 wages for this period, then pays $500 dividends. Assume no other transactions or adjustments.

  1. Income statement: $3,000 - $1,000 = $2,000 net income.
  2. Equity statement: contributed capital $10,000; ending RE = $0 + $2,000 - $500 = $1,500. Total equity $11,500.
  3. Cash flows: operating +$2,000; investing -$6,000; financing +$10,000 + $4,000 - $500 = +$13,500. Ending cash $9,500.
  4. Balance sheet: cash $9,500 + equipment $6,000 = $15,500 assets; debt $4,000 + equity $11,500 = $15,500.

Net income feeds retained earnings, ending RE feeds the balance sheet, and ending cash agrees between the cash-flow statement and balance sheet.

A cash receipt can increase a liability, an equity account, or revenue. Identify what the business has promised or delivered before deciding which one applies. Borrowing increases a liability and creates no revenue or expense.

Try it without notes

1. Choose the report

A lender wants total debt at 31 December; a manager wants a budget for opening a branch; an investor wants this year’s profit. Name the report or accounting branch for each.

Reveal solution and reasoning

Debt: balance sheet. Branch budget: managerial accounting. Annual profit: income statement (financial accounting). The first is a date; the third is a period.

2. Classify the accounts

Classify Prepaid Insurance, Unearned Revenue, Accounts Receivable, Wages Payable, Retained Earnings and Wages Expense. Which appear on the income statement?

Reveal solution and reasoning

Prepaid Insurance and A/R: assets. Unearned Revenue and Wages Payable: liabilities. RE: equity. Wages Expense: expense on the income statement; the others are balance-sheet accounts.

3. Follow retained earnings

Beginning RE is $18,000, revenue $40,000, expenses $29,000, dividends $4,000 and new share issuance $7,000. Calculate ending RE and the increase in total equity, assuming no other changes.

Reveal solution and reasoning

NI = $11,000. Ending RE = $18,000 + $11,000 - $4,000 = $25,000. Equity increases $7,000 contributions + $11,000 income - $4,000 dividends = $14,000.

4. Classify the cash flows

Classify cash paid for equipment, cash collected on A/R, loan principal repaid and dividends paid. Which of these necessarily creates a current-period expense?

Reveal solution and reasoning

Equipment: investing. A/R collection: operating. Principal repayment and dividends: financing. None necessarily creates a current-period expense: equipment is an asset; collection settles a receivable; principal settles debt; dividends distribute equity.
